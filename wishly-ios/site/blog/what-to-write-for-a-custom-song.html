What to Write for a Custom Song: Memories That Work

Updated 2026-10-09

What to write for a custom song starts with a familiar name, one specific memory, and the feeling you want to share. You do not need rhymes or a polished story. Describe what happened, what made it recognizable, and why it matters now, then explain the musical mood in ordinary language.

Which name should you include?

Use the name you call the person and would want them to hear in the song.

A nickname may feel more personal than a full legal name, provided the person likes it. Check spelling before submitting and make sure an unfamiliar nickname is understandable in context. You do not need to include identity documents, a home address, or unrelated personal information.

If accurate spoken pronunciation is important, check the service's actual way of handling that request rather than assuming a written spelling will settle it. The goal is recognition: the recipient should hear a name that belongs naturally in your relationship.

What kind of memory is easiest to work with?

Choose a scene you can describe simply.

Think about an ordinary moment with a clear detail: missing an exit on a drive, cooking a meal that went wrong, waiting together after a long day, or celebrating a small success. Explain where you were, what happened, and the detail you still remember. These are suggested prompts, not customer stories.

One clear scene gives the song a more coherent direction than a list of every holiday you have shared. If you include several memories, choose ones that support the same feeling. The size of the event matters less than how recognizable it is to the recipient.

How do you explain why the memory matters?

Add the meaning after the scene.

Did the person make you feel supported, help you laugh at a mistake, encourage a decision, or make a new place feel familiar? Describe that directly. Avoid relying only on words such as amazing, special, or perfect; they do not explain what this person did. You can be affectionate without exaggerating the relationship.

If the memory connects to a difficult period, keep the focus on what the recipient would welcome hearing. A song brief should not force someone to revisit private pain. Explain enough to guide the message while leaving out details that belong only in a private conversation.

What if you cannot think of anything to say?

Start with the last ordinary thing you did together, then ask what made it feel like the two of you.

Consider a recurring phrase, a regular meal, a habit, or a place you always stop. Write rough notes rather than waiting for a perfect paragraph. You might answer three questions: what happened, what detail would they recognize, and what do I appreciate about it? If nothing feels right, ask yourself what you would say in a short card.

The brief is material for a song, not a writing test. A sincere sentence with one real detail is more useful than an elaborate description that could fit anyone.

How do you describe music without knowing genres?

Use the way you want the song to feel: bright and playful, warm and relaxed, gentle and reflective, or energetic enough to dance to.

Describe instruments or voices only if you have a preference. You can mention acoustic guitar, piano, a fuller arrangement, or a soft vocal without knowing technical vocabulary. Listen to the service's examples to compare different directions. Do not request an exact copy of a famous recording or an imitation of a real singer's voice.

If you use an artist as a broad reference, explain the musical qualities you mean and check what the service permits. Plain descriptions help make the request clearer.

Which details should stay out of the brief?

Leave out passwords, contact information, private health details, financial information, and other material the song does not need.

Be careful with stories involving people who have not agreed to be included. An inside joke should make the recipient feel recognized rather than embarrassed, especially if the song may be played publicly. Do not include accusations, secrets, or a request for a particular emotional response. For photographs and recordings, check permission and the service's handling of uploads.

Keep a copy of the brief you are comfortable sharing. Personalization does not require revealing everything about someone; a small amount of appropriate detail can do the work.

What should you review before submitting?

Read the brief as though you were the recipient.

Are the name and facts correct? Is the occasion clear? Does the mood fit what you intend? Remove details that conflict or repeat the same point. Check the supported language, package files, delivery information, and how factual corrections are handled before purchasing. If you edit one part, check that the rest still makes sense.

You can use a simple structure: name, occasion, memory, meaning, musical mood, and anything important to avoid. Treat that as a guide rather than a mandatory long form. The finished brief should sound like your relationship, even if the wording remains informal.

Keep a final copy of the details you actually submitted, including any later changes.

This lets you compare the finished result with the intended facts without relying on memory. If you are requesting a correction, identify the exact issue and where it occurs: a name, a place, or an inaccurate event. Distinguish a factual problem from a new creative preference, and check how the service handles each.

Do not add several new memories while asking for one factual repair unless you mean to change the brief. Clear, limited feedback makes the request easier to understand and helps keep the original gift's purpose intact.

A short custom-song brief

“For Maya’s birthday. We met on our first day at work. She brought emergency snacks on every road trip and helped me move in the rain. I want her to feel appreciated. Upbeat, warm, easy to sing along to. Keep the jokes kind.” This is a suggested brief, not a customer order.
